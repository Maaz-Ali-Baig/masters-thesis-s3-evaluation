Settings of the series
----------------------
Object size series, concurrency 8, PUT and GET: 50 KiB, 1 MiB, 16 MiB, 100 MiB. Concurrency series, 1 MiB, PUT and GET: 1, 4, 16, 64.
Mixed workload (Warp default mix of GET, PUT, STAT, DELETE), 1 MiB, concurrency 16. GET datasets: 50000 objects of 50 KiB, 8192 of 1 MiB,
512 of 16 MiB, 80 of 100 MiB, prepared in the warm up run and read with --list-existing (Warp v1.8.0 spelling as detected by the script).
Before every GET run the script emptied the page cache on the three nodes over ssh (nodes handled: all three, see header; the success of each
drop was not logged, so it is set, not proven, for every run). Before every run the script checked the free space on all data disks
(limit 50 percent used; the script prints a "space:" line when it waits, not yet searched for in the log). The bucket was emptied after every
configuration.

PROVEN (read from the tables)
-----------------------------
P1. Every recorded run finished without errors. The three runs of a configuration lie close together for most configurations
    (largest spread: GET 1 MiB at concurrency 64, 685.32 to 787.00 MiB/s; PUT 1 MiB at concurrency 8, 80.83 to 87.35).
P2. Latency and throughput agree with the concurrency (Little's law, concurrency divided by average latency, my own arithmetic
    on the table values): PUT 1 MiB concurrency 1: 1 MiB / 53.0, 51.2, 51.0 ms = 18.9, 19.5, 19.6 MiB/s against 18.87, 19.54, 19.66;
    PUT 1 MiB concurrency 64: 64 / 467.0 ms = 137.0 against 136.93; GET 1 MiB concurrency 64: 64 / 87.7 ms = 729.7 against 720.96;
    GET 100 MiB concurrency 8: 800 MiB / 0.8491 s = 942 against 933.10. The values are consistent, so the tool measures what it says.
P3. PUT is slower than GET in every configuration: at the median 4.3 times at 100 MiB (216.17 against 933.10), 4.6 times at 16 MiB, 6.8
    times at 1 MiB and concurrency 8, and 10.8 times at 50 KiB (7.49 against 80.52 MiB/s, 153 against 1649 objects per second).
P4. The average PUT latency is about 51 to 58 ms for one 1 MiB object at concurrency 1 and for a 50 KiB object at concurrency 8 (52 ms
    average), while the average GET latency is 5 to 6 ms for 50 KiB and 11 ms for 1 MiB at concurrency 1.
P5. PUT throughput at 1 MiB grows with concurrency from 19.54 (1) to 109.36 (16) and 130.29 (64) MiB/s; GET grows from 90.35 (1) to
    708.01 (16) and 720.96 (64). Between 16 and 64 the median gain is 19 percent for PUT and 2 percent for GET (the GET ranges overlap).
P6. At 16 MiB and 100 MiB objects PUT is about 200 to 216 MiB/s and GET about 922 to 933 MiB/s at concurrency 8.

NOT PROVEN, hypotheses with the test that settles them (not to be written as findings)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
H1. The fixed cost of about 50 ms per PUT comes from the write path of the erasure set (writes to six drives, flush before the
    acknowledgement). Settles: the sar data of the three nodes (disk wait and write rate per run, already recorded) and a read of the
    RustFS configuration and documentation for its sync behaviour; the durability settings of the four systems are still open.
H2. The GET results are partly served from the page cache. Reason: each GET run reads far more data than the dataset (about 56 GiB in 60 s
    at 930 MiB/s against a dataset of about 8 GiB), and the cache was emptied only at the start of a run. Settles: disk read rate per run in
    the sar logs of the three nodes against the client throughput.
H3. The PUT to GET ratio comes from erasure coding work (encoding on a CPU without AVX2) and write amplification (about 2.0 times raw,
    record R1). Settles: CPU use per node during PUT and GET runs in the sar logs.
H4. The GET curve flattens above concurrency 16 because of a limit in the nodes or the client node (ceph2 is both). Settles: sar CPU of
    ceph2 and of the other nodes at concurrency 64.

LIMITS OF THIS SERIES (to be repeated in the thesis)
----------------------------------------------------
L1. The load node ceph2 is also a storage node (decision of 10 October, nobody asked for an extra VM). The client CPU cost falls on ceph2.
L2. Redundancy differs between the systems (RustFS erasure coding 3+3 against three copies in the others), so write costs differ by design.
L3. Three runs per configuration: ranges only, no statistical test. One cluster, one day.
L4. Durability and flush settings of RustFS are not read yet (H1).
L5. The control measurements before the series (dd of 2 GiB with direct writes and fsync on the first data disk: ceph0 1.33831 s, ceph1
    1.54475 s, ceph2 0.768667 s; iperf3 from ceph2 to ceph0 5.05 Gbit/s over 10 s with 495 retransmits) were read from the screen, not
    checked by hash. iperf3 is not installed on ceph1, so the network to ceph1 was not measured. The controls AFTER the series are not done.
L6. The laptop results for RustFS (beta.8) are not comparable with this series (different version, one node, laptop).
L7. The sar logs of the three nodes exist but are not analysed yet (H1 to H4 depend on them).

UPDATE (10 October 2026, later): limit L7 (sar logs not analysed) is answered in results/rustfs-vm-performance-sar-2026-10-10.txt. The status of H1 to H4 is
stated there: H2 is supported for 1 MiB and 50 KiB GET (most data came from memory) and not for 16 and 100 MiB; H4 is open (large GET: the receive side of the
load node was at 8.35 Gbit/s, consistent with a network limit but not proven); PUT plateau at 1 MiB is not explained by CPU, iowait or network; three
configurations (PUT 100 MiB, PUT 16 MiB, GET 50 KiB) had the load node above 80 percent CPU and are flagged as client limited by the rule of the method.
