PROVEN (read from the tables, arithmetic mine)
----------------------------------------------
P1. Every recorded run finished without errors. The three runs of a configuration lie close together for most configurations. Largest
    spreads: GET 1 MiB at concurrency 64, 1054.96 to 1285.79 MiB/s (p99 latency 147 to 367 ms), PUT 1 MiB at concurrency 8, 177.46 to 185.21.
P2. Latency and throughput agree with the concurrency (Little's law, concurrency divided by average latency):
    PUT 1 MiB concurrency 1: 1 MiB / 28.5, 27.8, 29.1 ms = 35.1, 36.0, 34.4 MiB/s against 34.88, 36.14, 34.08;
    PUT 1 MiB concurrency 64: 64 / 294.3, 303.3, 292.6 ms = 217.5, 211.0, 218.7 against 209.89, 210.75, 216.07;
    GET 100 MiB concurrency 8: 800 MiB / 0.6381, 0.6062, 0.6172 s = 1253.7, 1319.7, 1296.2 against 1277.36, 1313.76, 1302.15;
    GET 1 MiB concurrency 64: 64 / 55.7, 50.2, 62.3 ms = 1149, 1275, 1027 against 1240.18, 1285.79, 1054.96 (first run 7 percent off).
    The values agree within 8 percent, so the tool measures what it says.
P3. PUT is slower than GET in every configuration, at the median: 5.6 times at 100 MiB (231.81 against 1302.15), 5.6 times at 16 MiB,
    6.1 times at 1 MiB and concurrency 8, 5.2 times at 1 MiB and concurrency 1, 4.2 times at 50 KiB (46.14 against 193.46 MiB/s;
    945 against 3962 objects per second).
P4. Average latency of one request: PUT 1 MiB at concurrency 1 about 28 ms (27.8 to 29.1), GET 1 MiB at concurrency 1 about 5.5 ms (5.3 to
    5.6); at 50 KiB and concurrency 8: PUT 8.3 to 8.7 ms, GET 2.1 to 2.2 ms. GET 100 MiB at concurrency 8: 606 to 638 ms, PUT 100 MiB:
    3400 to 3494 ms.
P5. PUT throughput at 1 MiB grows with concurrency from 34.88 (1) to 120.96 (4), 183.46 (8) and 212.12 (16) MiB/s and does not grow
    above 16 (210.75 at 64, a change of -0.6 percent, ranges overlap). GET grows from 182.12 (1) to 705.61 (4), 1125.51 (8) and
    1283.49 (16) and is 1240.18 at 64 (-3 percent, the ranges overlap).
P6. At 16 MiB and 100 MiB PUT is about 232 to 234 MiB/s and GET about 1302 to 1303 MiB/s at concurrency 8, which is near the 1 MiB values at
    concurrency 16 and above (PUT 212, GET 1283). PUT 1 MiB at 16 users is about 9 percent below the large object PUT.

SPACE (observations about SeaweedFS disk space, made while preparing and running the series)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Deletes free no disk space in SeaweedFS until the master runs a vacuum: after the bucket is emptied the data stay in the volume files. Therefore
the script was changed (commit c116f33): before the wait for free space it calls on the first node "curl -s -L -m 300 -o /dev/null -w
'http %{http_code} in %{time_total} s' 'http://<node>:9333/vol/vacuum?garbageThreshold=0.01'" and sleeps 10 s. Outer log of the series: 19 calls
answered "http 200" and 14 calls ended after 300 s without an answer ("http 000"); 33 calls in total, the first 19 answered, the later 14 did not.
Why the later calls did not answer is NOT known (not examined). The series was not affected: free space was checked by the script before every run
and the later runs started. Earlier, on an idle cluster, one call took about 120 s. Also not examined: SeaweedFS used about 1.3 to 1.5 times the
logical data size per node in the first tests of 12 October, empty folders for the prefixes remain in the filer after deletes (0 objects).

NOT PROVEN, hypotheses with the test that settles them (not to be written as findings)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
H1. The fixed cost per PUT (28 ms for 1 MiB at concurrency 1) comes from synchronous writing of three copies through the filer and the volume
    servers. Settles: the SeaweedFS documentation or source for the write path (filer chunking, volume server fsync default) and the sar
    results (resource record, S1 shows disk write volume below three times the client volume).
H2. The GET results are partly served from the page cache. The resource record S2 supports this for every GET configuration (disks delivered
    15 to 17 percent of the client volume at 1 MiB, concurrency 8 and above). Not a hypothesis any more for 1 MiB, see the resource record.
H3. The PUT plateau of about 210 to 234 MiB/s (1 MiB from 16 users, 16 and 100 MiB at 8) has one common cause. The load node ceph2 is the busiest node in 16 of 17
    configurations (resource record S4) and above 90 percent CPU for 16 and 100 MiB, but at 69 to 73 percent for 1 MiB. Settles: a run with
    the load generator on a fifth VM (not available) or the per core CPU of ceph2 (sar -P ALL, not recorded).
H4. The replication to three copies is done synchronously by the filer or volume server before the acknowledgement (the setting 002). Settles:
    the source or a read back test with a node stopped right after an acknowledged write (planned fault tolerance run S1).

LIMITS OF THIS SERIES (to be repeated in the thesis)
----------------------------------------------------
L1. The load node ceph2 is also a storage node (decision of 10 October). The client CPU cost falls on ceph2, and part of the GET data of ceph2 is read
    locally and does not pass its network card (resource record S3).
L2. Redundancy differs between the systems (SeaweedFS three copies by replication 002, RustFS erasure coding 3+3, Garage three copies, Ceph as it will be configured), so write
    costs differ by design. This record contains no comparison between systems and no ranking.
L3. Three runs per configuration: ranges only, no statistical test. One cluster, one series, three hours on one night.
L4. The disk space handling (vacuum calls, 14 of 33 timed out) is part of the series protocol for SeaweedFS and changed the timing between runs;
    its effect on the results was not measured.
L5. Control measurements BEFORE the series (12 October, read from the screen, no hash): dd of 2 GiB with direct writes on disk1 of each node 662 MB/s
    (ceph0), 1.1 GB/s (ceph1), 905 MB/s (ceph2); iperf3 from ceph2 to ceph0 8.80 Gbit/s (10 October: 5.05 Gbit/s with 495 retransmits, so one stream
    varies between days); iperf3 is not installed on ceph1, so the network to ceph1 was not measured. The dd numbers differ from the ones of 10 October
    (1.34 s, 1.54 s and 0.77 s for 2 GiB, which is about 1.6, 1.4 and 2.8 GB/s, my arithmetic; whether the two dd commands were identical was not
    compared here). If they were identical, the disk speed of the VMs varied between days, which would be a limit for every comparison between systems
    (to check by repeating the controls on the day of each system). The controls AFTER the series are not done.
L6. The laptop results for SeaweedFS (earlier versions) are not comparable with this series (different version, one node, laptop).
L7. The series crossed midnight; the script perf-sar.sh had a bug for windows across midnight (fixed in 4ba7831, the resource record uses the fixed
    version); the Warp tables do not use the sar windows and are not affected.
